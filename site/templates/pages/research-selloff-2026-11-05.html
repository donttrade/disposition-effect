2. What we measured, and why there

Stock brokers do not publish what their customers do. One venue publishes every trade: Hyperliquid, a crypto exchange whose trades are recorded on a public blockchain. Besides crypto, it lists perpetual futures, which are contracts with no expiry date that are usually traded with borrowed money, called leverage. Some of its markets are listed by outside builders (Hyperliquid calls them HIP-3 markets) and carry labels such as Micron, AMD and the S&P 500. We call them builder-listed markets and name the largest as they are listed. When a leveraged position loses enough, the exchange closes it automatically.

Wallet
One blockchain address that trades on the exchange. It is not a person: one person can hold several, and some wallets are bots or funds. This page says “wallets”, never “traders”.
Active wallet
Any address that bought or sold at least one perpetual on that UTC day. It includes automated wallets such as bots and market makers, and, like every wallet here, it is not a person.
Force-closed (liquidated)
The exchange closed all or part of a leveraged position by itself, because its losses had used up the money backing it. The wallet did not pick the moment. In the data, a trade carries a liquidation flag, and the force-closed wallet is the one that took the market order in that trade. We count that flag and nothing else: other forced reductions are not counted.

We counted force-closed wallets on {{ F.event_short }} and compared the count with each of the 30 days before ({{ F.base_first }} to {{ F.base_last }}). We wrote the method down and hashed it before opening the data. The time is from our own clock; the hash is published and a dated deposit is pending. The plan and its hash.

What this can and cannot say. We did not compare with wallets that were not force-closed on the same day, so a busier day may explain part of the gap. The data also show an order, not its purpose. A new order may open a fresh position, close another one, or be a stop order placed earlier. The data carry no field that separates them. Studies of other groups have found more risk-taking after losses that have not been closed (Imas, 2016) and after morning losses (Coval and Shumway, 2005). This count does not test that: it counts orders, not risk.

5. Limits

  • A wallet is an address, not a person. Some are bots or funds, and one person can hold several. This page says “wallets”, never “traders” or “retail”.
  • This is one crypto venue. It is not the US stock market or its retail investors, and it says nothing about them.
  • We identify the builder-listed markets by the dataset’s builder field. We did not check what each market tracks, so we do not say what share of the value was in stock or index contracts. The largest are listed as {{ sell.top5_names }}.
  • Days are UTC days (7 p.m. to 7 p.m. New York time on {{ F.event_short }}).
  • The comparison is a rank among 31 days. It is not a test of cause.
  • A wallet force-closed in the last hour of a UTC day is left out of the 15-minute and 60-minute shares, so the same rule applies on every day.
  • The day after, 6 November, is not included. Its file had not been published when this was written. It will be reported separately.
