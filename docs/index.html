donttrade · the disposition baseline

Most traders sell their winners and keep their losers. Here is the number.

A published, reproducible baseline for one measurable habit — so that anyone measuring it on their own book has something to compare against.

76.1%

of 4,659 retail traders realised gains more readily than losses

That figure comes from a replication of Odean (1998) on a public, CC0-licensed panel of Chinese retail traders from Harvard Dataverse: a 4,731-trader panel covering 2,780,879 trader-stock-lot-days, June 2016 to March 2018. Of those, 4,659 held at least one winner and at least one loser on some day — which is what it takes for both halves of the measure to be defined.

3,547 of those 4,659 showed the effect. The code is public and runs from a clean checkout.

What is actually being measured

Two proportions, and then the ratio between them. Nothing more.

on every day a trader sold something PGR = realised gains ÷ (realised gains + paper gains)
PLR = realised losses ÷ (realised losses + paper losses)

ratio = PGR ÷ PLR

In plain terms: of all the winning positions you were holding on a day you sold something, what fraction did you actually sell? Then the same question for losing positions. If you sell winners more readily than losers, the first number is bigger than the second and the ratio is above 1.

The day condition matters. It only counts days you sold something, so a trader who simply does not trade much is not counted as disciplined. That is Odean's rule and it is the one most informal versions get wrong.

How to read your own number

> 1.0 You close winners more readily than losers. This is the common case — three quarters of the traders in the panel sat here.
≈ 1.0 You close winners and losers at about the same rate. Uncommon, and what a rule followed consistently tends to look like.
< 1.0 You cut losers more readily than winners. Rare. Worth checking it is not an artefact of a small number of trading days.

A ratio above 1 is not proof you are losing money. It is a description of one habit, measured one way. Whether it costs you depends on your strategy, your holding period and your costs — none of which this measures.

And a single trader's ratio is noisy. The baseline above is an average across thousands of people; one account over a few months can land anywhere by chance.

Why the baseline is public

A number is only useful if you can check it. Everything behind the figure on this page is open: the dataset is CC0, the code is MIT licensed, and the README's longest section lists what is wrong with the study, in order of how much it matters — including that the archive's two files are nested rather than independent, which moves the result.

Two other studies sit in the same repository. One of them came out negative — twenty years of SEC Form 4 insider clusters where the long-run edge turned out to be concentrated in a single crisis year — and was published as written. A third, a pre-registered test of volatility-scaled momentum, failed its own kill criteria at retail position sizes. The pre-registration, the failure and the corrections are all in the repository.

There is nothing to sign up for here and no data to submit. The baseline is the product of this page.

Running it yourself

The measurement script is public, is two files of standard-library Python with no network calls, and takes a five-column CSV exported from your own records. It prints aggregate totals to a terminal and nothing leaves the machine it runs on.
