Glossary

The handful of terms the studies use, in plain words, each with its source.

Disposition effect
The tendency to sell positions that are up more readily than positions that are down. Named by Shefrin and Statman (1985); measured on brokerage records by Odean (1998). Source: Shefrin & Statman (1985); Odean (1998)
PGR — proportion of gains realised
In Odean’s method: on the days a trader sells something, the share of their winning positions they actually sold, realised gains ÷ (realised gains + paper gains). The figures in this site’s public-panel study count every day a position is held instead, so there PGR is a daily rate: the share of held winning positions closed that day. Levels from the two conventions are not comparable; ratios are approximately comparable. Source: Odean (1998)
PLR — proportion of losses realised
The same share for losing positions: realised losses ÷ (realised losses + paper losses). If PGR is bigger than PLR, gains are being realised more readily than losses. The same two conventions apply as for PGR. Source: Odean (1998)
Paper gain / paper loss
A position that is up (or down) and was not sold that day. In Odean’s method only days on which the trader sold something else count; in this site’s public-panel study every held day counts. Source: Odean (1998)
Realised gain / realised loss
A position sold that day while it was up (or down). Source: Odean (1998)
Qualifying sale day
A day on which an account closed at least one position. In Odean’s method and in the client script (partner_disposition.py), only these days count, so a trader who rarely trades is not scored as disciplined. The public-panel study counts every held day instead; see PGR. Source: partner/ README
Orphan close
A closed position with no earlier day on which it was held as a paper position. A few are normal (positions opened and closed the same day); a majority means the export did not join correctly, and the script refuses it. Source: partner/ README
Denominator
Exactly who or what was counted underneath a percentage. Two honest studies can report very different levels because their denominators differ, which is why the ratio is the thing to compare. Source: this site’s studies
95% interval
A range that, by a stated method, would contain the true value in 95% of repeated samples. It shows how precisely the data pins a number down. Source: NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook
t statistic
An estimate divided by its standard error. Large values mean the estimate is far from zero relative to its noise; with very large samples it gets large even for small effects, so it is not a measure of size. Source: NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook
Form 4
The filing a company insider makes with the SEC after buying or selling the company’s shares. Source: Investor.gov

Trading and risk terms

The terms the free tools and the Learn pages use. Where a tool calculates the term, its formula here is the one the tool uses. The terms above are the ones the studies use, each with its source. Below, an entry has a source line where a published source exists; the others have no single formal definition and describe the term as traders use it.

Win rate
The share of closed trades that made money. Trades that closed at exactly zero are left out of both counts. Formula: win rate = winning trades ÷ (winning trades + losing trades) Measured in: Trade stats
Profit factor
Gross profit divided by gross loss, with the loss counted as a positive number. Above 1, the winning trades added up to more than the losing ones. With no losing trade there is nothing to divide by, and trade stats says so in words instead of showing a number. Formula: profit factor = gross profit ÷ gross loss Measured in: Trade stats
Expectancy
The average result per trade. In money it is the net result divided by the number of trades, which trade stats shows as the mean result per trade. In R it is the win rate times the average win in R, minus the loss rate times the average loss in R; the risk-reward calculator uses it with every win equal to the R multiple and every loss equal to 1R. Formula: expectancy in R = win rate × average win in R − loss rate × average loss in R Measured in: Trade stats, Risk-reward calculator
R multiple
A result measured in units of the risk taken. For a plan, it is the distance from entry to target divided by the distance from entry to stop. Formula: R = distance from entry to target ÷ distance from entry to stop Measured in: Risk-reward calculator
Break-even win rate
The win rate at which a given R multiple makes exactly zero before costs. Costs push the real figure higher. Formula: break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R) Measured in: Risk-reward calculator
Maximum drawdown
The largest fall from a running high to a later low in an account’s equity or balance, in money or as a share of that high. Trade stats and the drawdown calculator measure it on the curve of closed trades; a platform that includes open positions can report a larger figure. Measured in: Drawdown and recovery calculator, Trade stats
Drawdown duration
How long a drawdown lasts: from the high that began it to the trade that brings the account back to that high. The drawdown calculator counts it in trades and in calendar days. A drawdown still open at the end of a file has no end yet. Measured in: Drawdown and recovery calculator
Recovery gain
The gain, on the smaller balance that is left, that undoes a fall of a given size. It is always larger than the fall, because the gain applies to what remains. Formula: recovery gain = d ÷ (1 − d), for a fall of d as a fraction of the high Measured in: Drawdown and recovery calculator
Position size
How many units or lots a trade is. For a plan built from a stop, it is the amount risked divided by what one lot loses at the stop. The lot size calculator shows the formula with your own numbers and the loss after rounding down to the broker’s lot step. Formula: position size = amount risked ÷ (stop distance × value per point for one lot) Measured in: Lot size calculator
Lot
A unit of trade size set by the instrument’s contract specification. In spot forex a standard lot is usually 100,000 units of the base currency, with mini and micro lots a tenth and a hundredth of that, but contract sizes differ by instrument and broker, and the broker’s own specification is the authority. Measured in: Lot size calculator
Point value (pip value)
The money value of a one-point move, or one pip, for one lot: the “value per point” the lot size calculator asks for. It depends on the instrument, its contract size and, in forex, the account’s currency. Brokers publish it in the contract specification. Measured in: Lot size calculator
Risk of ruin
The chance that an account falls to a stated limit, such as zero or a firm’s maximum loss, before reaching a target, given a win rate, the sizes of wins and losses, and the amount risked per trade. The usual formulas assume independent trades with fixed odds, which real records rarely meet exactly. The Challenge Readiness Check replays your own past days many times against a rule set and reports how often the maximum loss was hit. Measured in: Challenge Readiness Check
Loss aversion
The finding that a loss weighs more heavily than a gain of the same size. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) describe it as part of prospect theory. It is often offered as one reason for the disposition effect. Source: Kahneman & Tversky (1979) See also: Disposition-effect calculator
Sunk cost
Letting money, time or effort already spent, and not recoverable, weigh on a choice about what to do next. Arkes and Blumer (1985) tested it in experiments. In a trade file, how long losing positions were held is one place the pattern would show. Source: Arkes & Blumer (1985)
Revenge trading
Opening a new trade soon after a loss in order to win the money back, often at a larger size or outside the plan. There is no single formal definition. The donttrade test’s version is a trade opened within 15 minutes of the close of a losing trade, for files that have times of day. See also: For traders: the five habits
Tilt
A word borrowed from poker for playing worse after a loss or a bad run because of frustration. In a trade file, the measurable part is whether the trades after a run of losses differ from the others. See also: For traders: the five habits
Overtrading
Trading more often than your own plan or usual pace. Not the business sense of the word, where a company takes on more than its capital supports. One way to measure it is to compare trades per day with your own median.
Martingale
A sizing scheme that makes the next trade larger after a loss, classically by doubling it so that one win recovers every loss so far. Because each loss makes the next one larger, a run of losses grows the drawdown faster than the same run at a flat size would. See also: Drawdown recovery table
Averaging down
Adding to a losing position at a lower price, which lowers the average entry price and makes the position larger, so a further fall costs more.
Trailing drawdown
A loss limit measured from the highest balance, or equity, the account has reached, so the limit rises as the account does, instead of staying a fixed distance below the starting balance. Firms differ in what moves it and whether it stops rising at some level, so the firm’s published rule is the definition. Measured in: Challenge Readiness Check
Daily drawdown (daily loss limit)
A limit on how much the account can lose within one trading day, measured from the day’s starting balance or equity, or as a fixed amount. Firms differ in when a day starts, in a time zone and an hour, and in what counts toward the limit. Measured in: Challenge Readiness Check
Pre-registration
Writing down a study’s question, data and tests, with a date, before running them, so that results cannot be chosen afterwards. Several donttrade studies were pre-registered; each study page says how. See also: Research

No cookies. What this site stores and sends

This site sets no cookies. Visits are counted with Cloudflare Web Analytics and Umami Cloud, which also counts some button clicks; neither sets a cookie. It stores your light/dark choice in your own browser and, if you buy the donttrade test, your access key there too. For that test your browser sends our server only stripped trade numbers. The server does not keep them: of those numbers it keeps only a scrambled fingerprint of each trade row from your first result, to match a re-test to the same history. Your file itself is not uploaded. Card payments are handled by Stripe. Forms send what you type to Web3Forms, which emails it to us, and subscribing opens Substack; their privacy policies apply.
