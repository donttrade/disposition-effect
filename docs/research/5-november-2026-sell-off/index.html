5 November 2026: 9,054 wallets were force-closed on Hyperliquid’s builder-listed markets, about 4.7 times the median weekday

On the Nasdaq Composite’s largest one-day fall since April 2025, forced closes on the builder-listed markets of one crypto exchange ran at about 4.7 times the median weekday. We counted them with a method we wrote down first.

1. What happened that day

On Thursday 5 November 2026 the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% to 24,315.00, its largest one-day fall since 4 April 2025, and closed 11.9% below its record close of 6 October; from that close it needs a gain of 13.5% to get back to the record. The Nasdaq-100 fell 6.36% and closed 14.7% below its record, and needs 17.3%. The S&P 500 fell 4.36% and the Dow 3.44%. The VIX volatility index closed at 28.18, up 70%.

Reuters, Trading Day (Jamie McGeever), via Investing.com, reported on 5 November 2026 that the Philadelphia chip index fell 12%, its second-biggest fall on record, and linked the fall to fears that the Democrats' midterm wins would slow the building of AI data centres. (Reuters report; reported, not recomputed here.)

2. What we measured, and why there

Stock brokers do not publish what their customers do. One venue publishes every trade: Hyperliquid, a crypto exchange whose trades are recorded on a public blockchain. Besides crypto, it lists perpetual futures, which are contracts with no expiry date that are usually traded with borrowed money, called leverage. Some of its markets are listed by outside builders (Hyperliquid calls them HIP-3 markets) and carry labels such as Micron, AMD and the S&P 500. We call them builder-listed markets and name the largest as they are listed. When a leveraged position loses enough, the exchange closes it automatically.

Wallet
One blockchain address that trades on the exchange. It is not a person: one person can hold several, and some wallets are bots or funds. This page says “wallets”, never “traders”.
Active wallet
Any address that bought or sold at least one perpetual on that UTC day. It includes automated wallets such as bots and market makers, and, like every wallet here, it is not a person.
Force-closed (liquidated)
The exchange closed all or part of a leveraged position by itself, because its losses had used up the money backing it. The wallet did not pick the moment. In the data, a trade carries a liquidation flag, and the force-closed wallet is the one that took the market order in that trade. We count that flag and nothing else: other forced reductions are not counted.

We counted force-closed wallets on 5 Nov and compared the count with each of the 30 days before (6 Oct to 4 Nov). We wrote the method down and hashed it before opening the data. The time is from our own clock; the hash is published and a dated deposit is pending. The plan and its hash.

Reading the table

  • Across all markets, 5 Nov had the most force-closed wallets of the 31 days, but not by much: 8 Oct was 2% lower and 29 Oct 4% lower. By value it ranked 2: 283 million US dollars against a median of 57.5 million.
  • On the builder-listed markets it stood alone: 9,054 wallets (of 39,804 active there), 1.8 times the previous 30-day high (30 Oct, a Friday), 5.2 times the 30-day median (the pre-stated measure) and 4.7 times the median weekday. The value force-closed there, 141 million US dollars, was 10.4 times the 30-day median and 7.5 times the median weekday.
  • 73% of the day’s force-closed value (207 million US dollars, 14,335 wallets) fell inside US stock-market hours. The busiest 15 minutes were 10:00 a.m. to 10:15 a.m. New York time: 44.3 million US dollars, 3,811 wallets.
  • 96% of the value force-closed was in long positions, which are bets on a price rising. On a typical day in the 30 days before it was 66%; 5 Nov ranked 6 of the 31 days on this share, and the 30 days before ranged from 3% to 98%.

50 more markets had fewer than 10 force-closed wallets each. As the plan requires, they are not shown one by one; together they held 0.413 million US dollars.

  • Share of the value is the market’s part of the 141 million US dollars force-closed on builder-listed markets that day. Long share is the part of the market’s value that was in long positions.
  • Its median day is the middle value of that market’s own 30 days before (6 Oct to 4 Nov). A day with no force-close counts as 0, so a market that was new, or quiet at weekends, has a low median and a large “times its median”. A dash means its median day had no force-close at all. A day with 1 to 9 wallets in a market counts at its true value in that median, but it is not listed one by one, so for 50 of these 95 markets the median cannot be worked out from the CSV alone (its column median_uses_unlisted_days says “yes”). Running per_market.py on Ironflow’s files gives every median exactly.
  • What it tracks. We checked the 20 markets that each held at least 1% of the value, using the builder’s own public documents and Hyperliquid’s public market data. Builder’s docs means the linked page of the builder’s documents names what the market tracks, and its leverage limit and margin rule match Hyperliquid’s data. How each price is taken is in the downloadable file, with the page that states it, or marked “not checked”. Partly sourced means something is still open, so we keep the name as listed. Not checked means the market held less than 1% of the value. A name alone is never enough: the market listed as PURRDAT tracks a stock whose ticker is PURR. Of the 20: 19 found in the builder’s docs, 1 partly sourced, 0 with no source found. Checked on 7 November 2026 (UTC); the documents describe each market on that day. The downloadable file gives the builder’s own words, the link and the same status words for each.

How to check it yourself

You need Python 3 and about 5 GB of free space for a while. The script downloads Ironflow’s 31 daily files, one at a time, at the exact version this study read, and checks each file’s hash against our run log before it counts anything. Get per_market.py, then run:

python3 -m pip install duckdb==1.1.3
python3 -I per_market.py run ironflow-files per-market-days --delete
python3 -I per_market.py build per-market-days 5-november-2026-per-market.csv && sha256sum 5-november-2026-per-market.csv

Ironflow replaced 5 of these 31 files on 6 Nov 2026 at 23:24 UTC, after this study ran, so the script downloads the exact version we read (dataset revision 8e0c2f593fdbf324772db734747ccfce597f3b80); on the replaced files the builder-listed figures do not change, and across all markets 5 Nov becomes 18,815 wallets (we published 18,813) and 10 Oct 1,142 (we published 1,129).

If your copy matches ours, the last line prints this SHA-256: d68c058c0a8b03989f36b41eb1376ef73d6c9aeee6b9423c8b7881dcbe9d1c59. The build step stops if the markets do not add up to the study’s totals.

5. Fifteen minutes later

Of the 17,105 wallets force-closed before 23:00 UTC, 3,031 (17.7%) placed at least one new market order in the next 15 minutes, against 12.9% on the 30 days before. Within an hour: 29.2% (4,989 wallets) against 21.2%. The daily median of the 30 days was 12.2% at 15 minutes, with the middle half of days between 10.8% to 14.1%. One quiet day was higher: 10 Oct, with 22.0% of about 1,100 force-closed wallets.

What this can and cannot say. We did not compare with wallets that were not force-closed on the same day, so a busier day may explain part of the gap. The data also show an order, not its purpose. A new order may open a fresh position, close another one, or be a stop order placed earlier. The data carry no field that separates them. Studies of other groups have found more risk-taking after losses that have not been closed (Imas, 2016) and after morning losses (Coval and Shumway, 2005). This count does not test that: it counts orders, not risk.

6. Limits

  • A wallet is an address, not a person. Some are bots or funds, and one person can hold several. This page says “wallets”, never “traders” or “retail”.
  • This is one crypto venue. It is not the US stock market or its retail investors, and it says nothing about them.
  • We identify the builder-listed markets by the dataset’s builder field. When this page first went up we had not checked what each market tracks. We have now checked the largest against their builders’ own documents (Market by market), but not every market, so we do not say what share of the value was in stock or index contracts. The largest are listed as SOXL, MU, SP500, XYZ100 and AMD.
  • Days are UTC days (7 p.m. to 7 p.m. New York time on 5 Nov).
  • The comparison is a rank among 31 days. It is not a test of cause.
  • A wallet force-closed in the last hour of a UTC day is left out of the 15-minute and 60-minute shares, so the same rule applies on every day.
  • The day after, 6 November, is not included. Its file had not been published when this was written. It will be reported separately.

For journalists

On 5 November 2026, 9,054 wallets were force-closed on the builder-listed markets of Hyperliquid, a crypto exchange whose trades are public: about 4.7 times the median weekday, and the most of the 31 days studied. A wallet is an address, not a person. It is one crypto venue, not the US stock market.

Figures you can quote as written

  • On 5 November 2026, 9,054 wallets were force-closed on Hyperliquid’s builder-listed markets, about 4.7 times the median weekday of the 30 days before.
  • Across all markets, 18,813 wallets were force-closed on 5 Nov, the most of the 31 days studied, but a near-tie: 8 Oct was 2% lower.
  • The value force-closed on 5 Nov was 283 million US dollars, and 96% of it was long.
  • 17.7% of the wallets force-closed on 5 Nov placed a new market order within 15 minutes, against 12.9% on the 30 days before; we did not compare with wallets that were not force-closed that day.

To stay accurate

  • Say “wallets”, not “traders” or “retail investors”: a wallet is an address, and one person can hold several.
  • It is one crypto exchange, not the US stock market, and it says nothing about the stock market’s investors.
  • It is a rank among 31 days, not a test of cause.
