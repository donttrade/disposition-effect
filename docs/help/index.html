How much gain recovers a drawdown?

Divide the drawdown by one minus the drawdown. A 10 percent drawdown takes about an 11.1 percent gain to recover, 20 percent takes 25 percent, 30 percent takes about 42.9 percent, and 50 percent takes 100 percent.

From: Drawdown and recovery calculator, Questions

How is maximum drawdown calculated?

Track the running high of the account’s closed balance after each trade, then measure the largest fall from any high to a later low. The tool reports it in money and, with a starting balance, as a percentage of the high.

From: Drawdown and recovery calculator, Questions

Why does my platform show a different maximum drawdown?

Platforms often include open-trade equity, which moves between trades. A trade file holds closed trades, so this page measures closed-balance drawdown, which can be smaller.

From: Drawdown and recovery calculator, Questions

How is the length of a drawdown counted?

In trades, from the trade after the high up to and including the trade that brought equity back to the high. In calendar days, from the close of the trade that set the high to the close of the trade that recovered it. A drawdown that has not recovered is counted to the last trade in the file.

How is lot size calculated?

Lot size equals the amount you are prepared to lose divided by the stop distance times the value of one point for one lot. For example, a risk amount of 200 with a 40-point stop and 1 per point per lot gives 5 lots. The page shows this formula filled in with your inputs.

From: Lot size calculator, Questions

Where do I find the value per point for gold or an index?

Your platform’s contract specification lists the contract size and tick value for each symbol. In MT5, right-click the symbol in Market Watch and open Specification. The value per point can differ between brokers for the same symbol name, so use your own broker’s figure.

From: Lot size calculator, Questions

The calculator does not suggest one. How much you risk is your decision, and this page does not recommend any size. It only converts the amount you choose into lots.

From: Lot size calculator, Questions

Why is the loss at the rounded size lower than what I entered?

Brokers trade in fixed lot steps, often 0.01 lots. If the exact size falls between steps, the page rounds down and shows the loss at the rounded size. If even one step is larger than your risk amount, the page says so instead of rounding up quietly.

From: Lot size calculator, Questions

No. It sizes from the stop distance you enter. If you want costs included, add the spread to your stop distance; commission and slippage are not modelled.

Why are whole days replayed instead of single trades?

Daily loss limits depend on how losses bunch within a day. Replaying whole days keeps that bunching, while drawing single trades would spread a bad morning across many days and make daily limits look safer than they were.

How do you calculate risk-reward ratio?

Divide the distance from entry to target by the distance from entry to stop. An entry at 100, stop at 98 and target at 106 risks 2 to make 6, which is 3R.

From: Risk-reward calculator, Questions

At what win rate does a ratio break even?

Break-even win rate equals 1 divided by 1 plus the reward-to-risk ratio, before costs. At 1R it is 50 percent, at 2R about 33 percent, and at 3R 25 percent.

From: Risk-reward calculator, Questions

What is expectancy in R?

Expectancy is the average result per trade measured in units of risk. It equals win rate times average win in R, minus loss rate times average loss in R. This calculator counts every win as the R multiple and every loss as 1R. A positive figure means trades with those numbers make more than they lose on average.

From: Risk-reward calculator, Questions

Why is a realised ratio often lower than the planned one?

Exits rarely happen exactly at the stop or target. Early exits, partial closes, slippage and fees all move the realised figure. The trade stats page shows the average win and average loss your own trade file actually produced.

From: Risk-reward calculator, Questions

Is a higher risk-reward ratio always better?

Not on its own. A higher ratio usually comes with a lower win rate, and what matters is the combination. This page shows the arithmetic and does not recommend any ratio.

What is a good profit factor?

Profit factor is gross profit divided by gross loss; above 1 means the trades in the file made more than they lost. There is no universal good number, and a high figure from a small sample is often luck. If there are no losing trades the page says so instead of printing a number.

The tool counts a trade from flat to flat on each symbol, takes fees off the result when the file includes them, and leaves out positions still open at the end of the file. A note on the results says when rows were skipped or hedged positions were found.

Words the studies use

11 questions
Disposition effect

The tendency to sell positions that are up more readily than positions that are down. Named by Shefrin and Statman (1985); measured on brokerage records by Odean (1998). Source: Shefrin & Statman (1985); Odean (1998)

From: Glossary, Disposition effect

PGR — proportion of gains realised

In Odean’s method: on the days a trader sells something, the share of their winning positions they actually sold, realised gains ÷ (realised gains + paper gains). The figures in this site’s public-panel study count every day a position is held instead, so there PGR is a daily rate: the share of held winning positions closed that day. Levels from the two conventions are not comparable; ratios are approximately comparable. Source: Odean (1998)

From: Glossary, PGR — proportion of gains realised

PLR — proportion of losses realised

The same share for losing positions: realised losses ÷ (realised losses + paper losses). If PGR is bigger than PLR, gains are being realised more readily than losses. The same two conventions apply as for PGR. Source: Odean (1998)

From: Glossary, PLR — proportion of losses realised

Paper gain / paper loss

A position that is up (or down) and was not sold that day. In Odean’s method only days on which the trader sold something else count; in this site’s public-panel study every held day counts. Source: Odean (1998)

From: Glossary, Paper gain / paper loss

Realised gain / realised loss

A position sold that day while it was up (or down). Source: Odean (1998)

From: Glossary, Realised gain / realised loss

Qualifying sale day

A day on which an account closed at least one position. In Odean’s method and in the client script (partner_disposition.py), only these days count, so a trader who rarely trades is not scored as disciplined. The public-panel study counts every held day instead; see PGR. Source: partner/ README

From: Glossary, Qualifying sale day

Orphan close

A closed position with no earlier day on which it was held as a paper position. A few are normal (positions opened and closed the same day); a majority means the export did not join correctly, and the script refuses it. Source: partner/ README

From: Glossary, Orphan close

Denominator

Exactly who or what was counted underneath a percentage. Two honest studies can report very different levels because their denominators differ, which is why the ratio is the thing to compare. Source: this site’s studies

From: Glossary, Denominator

95% interval

A range that, by a stated method, would contain the true value in 95% of repeated samples. It shows how precisely the data pins a number down. Source: NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook

From: Glossary, 95% interval

t statistic

An estimate divided by its standard error. Large values mean the estimate is far from zero relative to its noise; with very large samples it gets large even for small effects, so it is not a measure of size. Source: NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook

From: Glossary, t statistic

Form 4

The filing a company insider makes with the SEC after buying or selling the company’s shares. Source: Investor.gov

From: Glossary, Form 4
