STUDY · PRE-REGISTERED · NEGATIVE RESULT · 17 OCT 2026

We tested 50 trading strategies, from swing trades to day trades. None survived.

This page reports a negative research result. It is not a strategy, a recommendation or a signal, and nothing on it says what anyone should hold.

The swing study’s 32 strategies plus 18 new rules: 7 investing rules judged against holding the market, 7 overnight rules and 4 day-trading rules on one-minute bars. Each new rule was sealed with a SHA-256 hash before any data was downloaded, and judged net of costs. None passed: 0 of 50 across the project, 0 of 44 under sealed pre-registration.

Pre-registered and sealed 16 Oct 2026 (investing and overnight 21:14:06 UTC; day trading 21:34:27 UTC) · Run 16–17 Oct 2026 · Published 17 Oct 2026 · Code: to be published with this study; the SHA-256 of every file is under “Reproduce it” · Data: Kenneth French data library; FRED; free vendor-adjusted daily ETF prices; Alpaca one-minute bars

This study combines two rounds of work. The first, published on 16 October as the swing-trading study, covered 32 distinct strategies across the project. The second round, run on 16–17 October, added 18 new rules: 7 investing rules, 7 overnight rules and 4 day-trading rules. That makes 50 distinct strategies. None of them passed. Of the 50, 44 were tested under sealed pre-registration; the other 6 were tested earlier under looser protocols, as the swing study explains. The section "How we counted" shows both ledgers line by line.

1. The short version

  • 50 distinct strategies, 0 survivors. 26 swing rules and 18 new rules were tested under rules sealed with a hash before any result was computed, which makes 0 of 44 under sealed pre-registration. Counting the 6 earlier strategies, it is 0 of 50 across the project.
  • The new round covered three styles. Seven monthly investing rules (trend timing, momentum, 60/40, value and momentum tilts) were judged against simply holding the US stock market. Seven overnight rules bought an ETF at the close and sold it at the next open, or the reverse. Four day-trading rules were tested on one-minute bars of SPY and QQQ, including the opening-range breakout (ORB) popular with futures traders.
  • The one investing rule that looked strong was a famous effect, re-measured. A big-cap momentum tilt beat the market’s Sharpe ratio before 2013 with a t-statistic of 3.93. Momentum was published as an anomaly decades before 2013, on that same history, so this was a re-measurement, not a discovery. After 2013 it did not beat the market: t −0.82 on the research portfolios and t −0.16 on the real momentum ETF, MTUM. MTUM also fell further than the S&P 500 at its worst (30.2% against 23.9%).
  • Overnight holding looked good before costs and failed after them. SPY held only overnight had a gross Sharpe ratio of 1.01 a year over 1993–2012, against 0.49 for holding it all the time. After an assumed 2 basis points per trade, its Sharpe fell to 0.04, and $1,000 became $977 instead of $4,690. All seven failed the cost test.
  • The opening-range breakout reproduced its gross edge and lost it to costs. On QQQ, 2016–2020, the rule made +3.56 basis points per trade before costs. Our assumed costs were 5.47 basis points per trade, so the net result was −1.85 basis points a day (t −1.21). An independent replication on five index CFDs, published before this test, found the same thing: gross edge reproduced, net about zero.

A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of one percent: 10 bp is 0.10%.

3. How we tested

The swing study explains the basic ideas (backtests, overfitting, multiple testing, alphas and Sharpe ratios) in plain words. This round used the same approach, with a stricter bar.

Sealed pre-registration

Every rule, data source, date split, cost and pass/fail test was written down before any data was downloaded. Each file was then fingerprinted with SHA-256 and the fingerprint recorded in SEAL-BROADEN.md. A fingerprint changes completely if one character of the file changes.

  • The investing (INV) and overnight (ON) pre-registrations were sealed at 21:14:06 UTC on 16 October 2026.
  • The day-trading (ID) pre-registration was sealed at 21:34:27 UTC the same day, after a probe that read only the earliest available timestamp of the data.

Every later clarification went into a separate, fingerprinted addendum. Wherever two readings of a rule existed, the worse verdict counted. Section 9 lists the addenda with their timestamps.

Discovery, then one-touch confirmation

Each family had a discovery period, used to decide which rules deserved a final test, and a locked confirmation period, used once for the verdict.

The day-trading split differs because the free one-minute history starts in January 2016.

The lock was enforced in code. The data loader refused any confirmation-dated row unless an unlock file held the exact fingerprint of the sealed pre-registration. Every read, allowed or refused, was logged. The unlock was consumed (renamed) just before the first confirmation row was parsed, so a second run was impossible. A crash after that point would have counted as a FAIL.

The bar: Bonferroni across k = 7

Seven confirmation slots were reserved for the whole round (INV up to 3, ON up to 2, ID up to 2), whether or not they were used. The Bonferroni correction divides the allowed error rate across those seven. That sets the t-statistic bar at 2.690, against 2.394 or 1.96 in the swing study, and the placebo bar at the 99.286th percentile. Only one slot was used.

To reach confirmation, a rule had to clear a discovery gate first: a t of at least 2.0, a placebo percentile of at least 95, a smaller drawdown than its benchmark (INV, ON) or at most 20% (ID), a cost cushion (ON, ID), and a minimum sample size.

Deflated Sharpe

The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey and López de Prado, 2014) asks how likely it is that a Sharpe ratio this high is real, given how many strategies were tried. It returns a probability between 0 and 1, and every confirmation needed at least 0.95. N was 50 for the investing and overnight rules and 54 for the day-trading rules.

Integrity gates before any verdict

Before discovery could run, each family’s machinery had to pass four checks:

  • Null test. 100 synthetic panels with exactly zero edge. A rule’s discovery gate could pass in no more than 10 of them.
  • Planted test. 100 synthetic panels with a known edge added. The gate had to pass in at least 95 of them.
  • Seal refusal. A read of a confirmation-dated row without the unlock file had to be refused and logged.
  • Look-ahead. The code had to assert that every decision used only data available at that moment.

Costs

Every result is net of costs, and every cost is an assumption, because none of the free data sources includes bid and ask quotes.

  • INV: 0.10% of traded value per side. The French research portfolios also pay an assumed fund-cost drag: 0.20% a year for value, 0.50% a year for momentum.
  • ON: two trades a day, at an assumed half-spread of 2 bp per side for SPY and QQQ, 3 bp for IWM, and 5 bp for the sector funds and XIU.TO.
  • ID: 2 bp per side for market orders, 4 bp for stop exits and 1 bp for limit targets.

Momentum is a known, published effect

Momentum was published as an anomaly by Jegadeesh and Titman in 1993. Carhart (1997) and the Fama-French size/momentum portfolios, which supplied INV7’s discovery data, were built and studied on the same pre-2013 history. INV7’s discovery t of 3.93 is therefore a re-measurement of a published effect on its own publication-era data. It is not an out-of-sample discovery, and we do not present it as one.

The only out-of-sample evidence here is the 2013–2026 confirmation. That period starts two decades after publication and uses an ETF launched to harvest the effect, so it tests whether the effect persisted after publication and whether a fund could capture it. It found no risk-adjusted advantage. Research on how anomaly returns decay after publication (McLean and Pontiff, 2016) is the relevant prior; it was not re-read for this study. The INV pre-registration also recorded the wider contamination risk in advance: anyone writing these rules knows, in broad terms, how US stocks, value and momentum behaved after 2012. Choosing only published default parameters, with no grid search, was the only defence.

Every overnight rule failed on cost. The breakeven column is the cost per side at which the rule stops beating buy-and-hold. For ON1–ON6 it ranged from 1.06 to 2.22 bp. The sealed bar required at least 2.5 times the assumed base cost, which is 5 to 12.5 bp. Where an overnight edge exists in this data, it is smaller than an assumed 2–5 bp half-spread. ON7, intraday-only SPY, lost money before any cost. None of the seven advanced, so the overnight confirmation data was never opened.

The gross pattern is striking. Over 1993–2012, a dollar in SPY held only overnight grew 7.27 times before costs, held only during the day it shrank to 0.65 of a dollar, and held all the time it grew 4.69 times. QQQ shows the same split (6.44, 0.21 and 1.35 times, 1999–2012). This matches the overnight premium reported for QQQ by Kelly and Clark (2011). It is also descriptive only: it ignores the cost of trading twice a day, and those costs erase it. QQQ overnight (ON2) came closest. It beat buy-and-hold even after costs (net growth 3.5% a year against 2.2%), but its edge was not statistically distinguishable from luck (t 0.30) and its cost cushion was 2.22 bp against the 5 bp required.

Each rule needed a net t of at least 2.0, a breakeven cost multiple of at least 2.5 and a drawdown of at most 20%. All four failed all three. ID2, ID3 and ID4 also failed the random-direction placebo, so their direction choices were no better than coin flips. Nothing advanced, so the day-trading confirmation data (2021–2026) was never opened.

ORB on QQQ is the notable result. It was the only rule whose direction clearly beat chance: its placebo percentile of 99.4 means it beat 99.4% of 1,000 random-direction versions of itself. Its gross edge of +3.56 bp per trade is real in this data. The assumed costs were 5.47 bp per trade, and the rule broke even at 0.65 times those costs. Even at half the assumed cost, the net mean was +0.80 bp a day with a t of 0.53, far from significant.

That is what an independent replication found before this test. Krueger (September 2026), replicating the same ORB paper on five index CFDs over 2015–2026, reported a gross average of +0.131 R per trade on the Nasdaq-100 (t 2.9) and a net average of +0.002 R (t 0.0). R is the risk per trade, the distance from entry to stop. His conclusion was "gross reproduced, net zero". Ours is the same, on different data and a different cost model.

A reproduction check run as part of the integrity tests confirms that the engine matches the paper. With the paper’s own sizing and its commission of $0.0005 per share, and no slippage, our 2016–2020 QQQ run gave 1,220 trades, a 23.9% win rate, +0.130 R per trade and a Sharpe ratio of 1.14. The paper reports 1,795 trades over 2016–2023, a 24% win rate, +0.13 R and a Sharpe ratio of 1.12. The gap between the paper and our verdict is the cost model: the paper assumed no slippage, and we charged spread and slippage.

The day-trading run happened after the internal Manager review. That review, written at 00:51 UTC on 17 October, recorded ID as "still NOT RUN". The ID integrity tests and discovery ran afterwards, under the ADDENDUM-4 rules, and the discovery output was sealed at 01:10:14 UTC.

7. Scalping: literature only

Scalping was declared untestable in the plan, before any data was read. A scalp’s edge is a few ticks, about the same size as the spread, the trader’s place in the order queue and the delay in reaching the exchange. One-minute bars cannot show whether a limit order would have filled or what a market order would have paid at that millisecond. An honest test needs tick-by-tick trades and quotes with a model of the queue, and the free data has neither.

What the research we read says (each source as read for this study):

  • Speed decides who wins. Baron, Brogaard, Hagströmer and Kirilenko (2019) find that relative latency explains large performance gaps between high-frequency trading firms, and that firms that got faster did better.
  • Most persistent day traders lose. Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti (2019) followed people who began day-trading Brazilian index futures in 2013–2015. Of the 1,551 who kept going for at least 300 days, 97% lost money, and 17 (1.1%) earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage. (We read a summary on Quantpedia, not the paper.)
  • Very few are predictably profitable. Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean (2014), using Taiwanese data for 1992–2006, find that less than 1% of the day-trader population predictably earns positive returns after fees. The top 500, ranked on the prior year, earned 61.3 bp a day before fees and 37.9 bp after.

These base rates apply with even more force to retail scalpers, whose edge per trade is smaller still.

8. What this means for a trader

These are facts from the runs above, not advice.

  • Costs decide short-horizon rules. Overnight SPY doubled the market’s gross Sharpe ratio and lost it all at 2 bp per trade. The ORB had a gross edge and lost it at 5.47 bp per trade. Before trusting any intraday backtest, check whether it charges the spread and slippage, not just commission.
  • A futures trader can compare our breakeven with their own costs. ORB on QQQ broke even when costs were 0.65 times our assumed 5.47 bp per trade: about 3.56 bp per round trip, the size of its gross edge. Your own all-in cost per round trip on the futures you trade is the number to compare with that. We do not know it.
  • A famous published edge is not a fresh one. Big-cap momentum was the strongest investing result before 2013 and had no risk-adjusted advantage after it, on research data or on the ETF built to capture it.
  • Smaller drawdowns are not proof of skill. The trend-timing rules roughly halved the worst fall over 1927–2012 (43.1% and 44.5% against 83.7%) but did not show a Sharpe advantage that could be told apart from luck.
  • Many prop firms rule out overnight holds anyway. Topstep, for example, states that it "does not permit holding positions from one session to the next". An overnight edge, even a real one, is out of reach for those accounts.
  • Low win rates are normal for breakout rules. ORB on QQQ won 23.0% of trades after costs and still came closest to working. Win rate alone says little about whether a rule makes money.
  • Buy-and-hold was the bar, and nothing beat it. Every investing rule was compared with simply holding the market. None did better on a risk-adjusted basis in a way that survived these tests.

9. The addenda: four gates redesigned, with timestamps

A sealed pre-registration cannot be edited, so every change was made in a new, fingerprinted addendum. Four of the integrity gates in Section 3 had been specified in a way that a correct engine could not pass, or passed too easily. They were redesigned. We say this plainly because it is the part of the process most open to abuse: a gate redesigned after seeing results can be bent to let a favourite through.

What did not change: no kill criterion, discovery threshold, date split, rule, cost, k or N was altered by any addendum. Each redesigned gate was calibrated on synthetic data, and for ADDENDUM-4 the acceptance rule was written down before the re-check ran.

What we cannot claim: ADDENDUM-3 was written after some real discovery numbers had been seen. They were Sharpe-difference point estimates for four timing rules, not gate results, and none of those rules later advanced. ADDENDUM-3 changed no outcome rule. But it did decide whether the investing family continued at all. The sealed text said INV halts if an integrity test fails, and the sealed null had failed. The lead’s written ruling (DISCLOSURE-1) is that a gate a correct engine fails by construction is a design defect and may be replaced, following the ADDENDUM-1 precedent. It notes that no INV7 statistic existed before ADDENDUM-3. A reader may judge that ruling differently. If INV had halted, INV7 would never have been confirmed, and the result, nothing passed, would be the same.

The lesson is recorded for the next pre-registration: run every integrity test on synthetic null and planted data before sealing.

10. Real-data exposures

A locked holdout means nothing if someone looks at it. These are every real-data read we know of outside the logged discovery and confirmation runs.

  • Coder (before any discovery): downloads, file hashes, structural counts and data-quality counts. For the day-trading data, it also read bar timestamps for both windows, including one direct read of every month’s timestamps (2021 onwards included) outside the logging loader. No price was read. Three scratch scripts that read real ID timestamps were kept outside the study folder and, at the time of the Manager review, had not been archived or hashed.
  • Tester, before ADDENDUM-3: the shift test computed real discovery Sharpe differences, unshifted and shifted, for INV1 (0.0311 and 0.0181), INV2 (0.0393 and 0.0036), INV3 (0.0638 and 0.0562) and INV5 (0.0242 and 0.0235). It also produced the sealed null’s pass counts using real moments, and pass counts from the sealed planted tests run on real data.
  • Tester diagnostic, before ADDENDUM-3: a script printed real discovery-period (1962–2012) stock and bond statistics: monthly mean and standard deviation 0.00869 and 0.04517 for stocks, 0.00603 and 0.02300 for bonds, a T-bill mean of 0.00418, and monthly excess Sharpe ratios of 0.0997 and 0.0803. Only their correlation (0.108) had been disclosed in ADDENDUM-3.
  • Tester seal attacks (16 Oct, 23:17 UTC and 17 Oct, 00:32 UTC): these opened confirmation sessions in scratch copies that held the real data. They read SPY daily rows through 2026-09-30 (8,475 rows, three times) and the French monthly market file through 2026-08 (1,202 rows, once). That file contains the market and T-bill series for INV7’s French confirmation. Only row counts were printed; no return or statistic was computed or shown. Future seal attacks run on synthetic data only.
  • Manager: the independent INV7 check read the French discovery rows (to 2012-12) directly from the zip files, bypassing the access log. No confirmation row was read.
  • The lead’s decisions file (M1–M3, written before any data was read) was sealed late, at 01:03:06 UTC on 17 October.
  • Earlier project runs: the overnight files' confirmation rows were read once on 16 October by the swing study’s C22 run, a close-based rule. No overnight or intraday statistic was computed from them, and the overnight confirmation was never opened.
  • The literature: the day-trading confirmation window overlaps the ORB authors' sample (to February 2023) and the noise-area authors' sample (to April 2024). It was unseen by this project, not by the literature. As it turned out, no day-trading rule reached confirmation.

11. Deviations, mismatches and other limitations

  • INV7’s cost criterion could not fail for cost reasons. K4 required a breakeven cost of at least 0.25% per side. A static tilt and its benchmark each pay a single initial purchase, so trading cost cannot change which one has the higher Sharpe ratio. In discovery the breakeven was reported as above 5%. In confirmation K4 is recorded as failed only because the Sharpe difference was already negative at zero cost. The real cost tests were the MTUM run, whose fund fees are inside its price, and the assumed drag on the French portfolio.
  • INV7 drag sensitivity. The French run charged an assumed 0.50% a year. On discovery data, the Manager’s separate recompute found that the advantage would vanish at a drag of 3.28% a year, and the t-statistic would fall below 2.0 at 1.87% a year. This was information only and not a trial.
  • Cost timing (disclosed deviation). The INV pre-registration says a trade’s cost is charged "in the month the trade happens". The code charges it in the month the new weights are held. Total cost is identical except for the final trade. The lead ratified this in ADDENDUM-1, before any discovery run, and the literal reading was not implemented.
  • Python 3.11 only. The pre-registrations name Python 3.9. No 3.9 interpreter was available, and the installed numpy and pandas need 3.11 or later. The code was checked for 3.9 compatibility at syntax and API level only, and every run used Python 3.11.
  • The French T-bill source changed in 2024. From June 2024 the French library’s risk-free rate comes from the ICE BofA 1-month T-bill index instead of Ibbotson, according to the file header. Every INV comparison subtracts it from both legs, so it shifts both Sharpe ratios together. Its effect was not separately measured.
  • The French portfolio is not MTUM’s index. BIG HiPRIOR is the value-weighted top third of big-cap stocks by prior 12-month return. MTUM tracks MSCI’s risk-weighted momentum index. The tradable run also starts four months later (2013-05, MTUM’s first full month) and ends one month later than the French run. Both had to pass, so the mismatch could only make passing harder.
  • Costs are assumed, not measured. No quote data was available for any family. One-minute bars are consolidated last-sale prices, not quotes, and stop fills in fast markets may be worse than 4 bp.
  • ETFs stand in for futures. Prop-firm traders mostly trade index futures. We tested SPY and QQQ, and their breakeven costs in basis points are the comparison point.
  • Low power for investing rules. About 164 months is short for a Sharpe-difference test at a bar of 2.690. A FAIL here does not show a rule is harmful. It shows the rule is not proven better than holding the market.
  • Not checked here: whether a Canadian broker supports market-on-open and market-on-close orders, the currency cost of buying US-listed ETFs, and the tax treatment of frequent trading. None of these mattered, because no rule passed.
  • This is not proof that no strategy works. These 50 rules, as written, on this data, with these costs, did not pass these tests. Strict tests trade some missed real edges for far fewer false ones.
