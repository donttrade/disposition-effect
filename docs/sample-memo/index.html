SPECIMEN

Sample memo and method

Method

How the figures are computed: the scripts and data, the glossary, the export guide for a firm’s own records, and the client script, partner_disposition.py.

Specimen memo, computed on public data

Read this first. This specimen uses the public study’s script (reproduce.py). A client memo is produced by partner_disposition.py and contains pooled PGR, PLR, their ratio and a t statistic, with counts. It has no share of traders and no per-trader ratio. Its only intervals are for the age-controlled figures. See the client script’s real output on a synthetic file ↓
Specimen. Not a client engagement. No firm commissioned or saw it. Every figure comes from the published disposition-effect study.
Question
In this panel, what share of traders realised gains more readily than losses, counting every day a position was held?
Figure
76.1% (3,547 of 4,659 traders; Chinese A-share social-trading portfolios, 2016–18)
95% interval
74.9–77.3%, treating traders as independent; the study explains why they are not quite
Denominator
Traders with at least one winner and at least one loser on some day, so that both halves of the measure are defined: 4,659 of the 4,731 in the panel
Supporting figures
Per trader then averaged: PGR 0.1925, PLR 0.1200, ratio 1.61. Pooled across every observation: PGR 0.0785, PLR 0.0535, ratio 1.47
Period
24 Jun 2016 to 27 Mar 2018
Observations
2,780,879 trader-stock-lot-days across 3,501 stocks; every day a position was held counts
Data
Jin, Li & Zhu, replication files for PLoS ONE (2021), CC0, Harvard Dataverse
Script
reproduce.py in the public repository. Client engagements use partner_disposition.py, which computes pooled PGR and PLR from a five-column extract, counting only days on which an account sold something, as Odean did; since 17 Oct 2026 it also makes the same comparison with holding age held fixed. It does not produce a share of traders or a per-trader ratio. The like-for-like public figure is the pooled ratio, 1.47, and even that is only approximately comparable, because the two count days differently
What this does not show
  • It measures the problem, not a fix. Nothing here says whether anything corrects the disposition effect.
  • Chinese A-shares, mid-2016 to early 2018, one social-trading platform. Whether it holds for North American retail today is an assumption, not a result.
  • Social-trading users are a selected population. Xueqiu cube operators chose to run public portfolios.
  • I did not collect this data. It is someone else’s replication file, released CC0. The contribution is the computation and publishing it.
  • The pooled and per-trader ratios differ, 1.47x against 1.61x, because the denominators differ. Both are above 1. Quoting one without the other is picking.
  • Significance is not effect size. The t-statistics are enormous because n is enormous. Cohen’s d of 0.49 is the honest magnitude of this measure: moderate, not overwhelming. The study’s robustness update shows the size also depends on how “gain” is timed.
  • No minimum activity was required. A trader with one winning and one losing held day counts the same as one with thousands; how sensitive the share is to a minimum-activity rule has not been computed.
  • Traders whose two rates are exactly equal are counted as not showing the effect; reproduce.py does not report how many there are.
  • The source data’s gain flag is 1 only when a position was in profit, so a break-even day counts on the loss side. The client script, partner_disposition.py, drops break-even rows instead.
  • Holding age: positions are sold less often the longer they are held. A robustness update to the study (17 Oct 2026) finds the direction survives when holding age is held fixed, but the size depends on how “gain” is timed, and a V-shaped selling pattern is not ruled out.
Reliance
A client memo is for the client’s use only; no other party may rely on it.

HOW TO READ THE AGE-CONTROLLED FIGURES
- Positions are sold less often the longer they are held. If losing
  positions are on average older than winning ones, the pooled ratio at
  the top can show an effect that is only an age pattern. The within-age
  ratio compares winners and losers of the same holding age; use it, not
  the pooled ratio, for the size of the effect.
- Holding age is counted from each position's first row in this file.
  Positions already open on the file's first date are left out of these
  figures because their age is unknown; the pooled figures still use them.
- The fixed-effects line compares each account with itself, at the same
  holding day. It is a different average from the pooled ratio, so the two
  are not expected to match.
- Gain timing: a position's gain or loss on the day it is sold may partly
  reflect the sale itself (the fill or the closing mark). The lines that
  take the gain or loss from the position's previous row avoid that. If
  they are much smaller, the effect depends on same-day timing.
- Not ruled out: traders may sell large moves in either direction more
  often (a V-shaped pattern, Ben-David and Hirshleifer 2012). Separating
  that from a preference for selling winners needs the size of each gain
  or loss relative to the position, which these five columns do not carry.
- The intervals here are clustered by account (the within-age ratio) and
  by account and date (fixed effects). The t statistic at the top treats
  every row as independent and is not.
- NOT MEASURED means the data cannot support the figure, never zero.

How to read the age-controlled figure

The headline ratio compares the share of winning positions you sold with the share of losing positions you sold, pooled over every position however long it had been held. Positions are usually sold less often the older they get, so if losers tend to be older, part of the headline is an age pattern, not a habit. The within-age ratio makes the same comparison only between positions of the same age (same number of days since opened), then combines the days. That is the figure to use for the size of the effect. The table by holding age shows where the effect sits: often mostly in positions held a day or less. The fixed-effects ratio compares each account with itself at the same holding age; it is a different average and is not expected to equal the pooled one. If the within-age interval includes 1, the data do not show an effect once age is held fixed. NOT MEASURED means the data cannot support that figure; it never means zero.
